Understanding your procedure

Neck fusion.
Made easier to understand.

ACDF is short for anterior cervical discectomy and fusion. Let’s unpack what that means, what the procedure is intended to do, and what to ask at your visit.

01 / The anatomy

A small space.
An important job.

A disc sits between these two neck bones. Changes in a disc or nearby bone can put pressure on a nerve root or the spinal cord.

Your symptoms, examination, and imaging help your clinician understand whether pressure is present and whether it explains what you’re feeling.

Simplified concept. Technique and implants vary.

02 / The procedure

Remove pressure.
Support the space.

Through an approach from the front of the neck, the surgeon removes the affected disc and material causing pressure. This is the discectomy part.

Bone graft, often held in a spacer, is placed in the space. A plate and screws or another fixation design may be used to support the treated level while it heals.

Intended healing over time, not an immediate or guaranteed result.

03 / The intended goal

Room for the nerves.
Stability as bone heals.

The aims are to relieve pressure on nerve tissue and allow the treated vertebrae to join through bone healing. This is the fusion part.

The fused level no longer moves as it did before. Healing takes time; symptom improvement and successful fusion are not guaranteed. Your surgeon will explain what is realistic in your case.

The decision matters

Why this procedure?
Why now?

Those are fair questions. Understanding the recommendation is part of understanding your care.

ACDF may be discussed when a condition in the neck is putting pressure on nerve tissue and the findings fit your symptoms and examination.

Appropriate nonsurgical options may include medication, physical therapy, or injections. They are not suitable for every condition. Significant spinal cord compression or worsening neurological problems may change the timing of surgical discussions.

Surgery is a decision about your individual situation, not a required next step in this guide.

The tools behind your care

What stays. What heals.

Implants support a treatment plan. They do not replace the body’s healing process.

01

Spacer

A spacer may help support the space between the treated vertebrae and hold graft material. Its size and design depend on the procedure.

02

Bone graft

Graft material supports the process of forming a bony connection. Your surgeon can explain the planned graft source and its considerations.

03

Fixation

A plate and screws or another fixation design may help stabilize the level while fusion develops. Not every ACDF uses the same construct.

The illustrations show generic concepts, not a specific manufacturer’s product. Your surgeon determines the appropriate implants and graft for your procedure.

A little deeper

Good questions deserve clear answers.

What are the risks and limitations?

All surgery has risks. ACDF risks can include swallowing difficulty or voice changes, infection, bleeding, nerve or spinal cord injury, failure of the bones to fuse, implant problems, and persistent or recurrent symptoms. Further surgery may be needed.

This is not a complete list. Ask about the risks most relevant to you, what may affect healing, and how your care team manages them.

What alternatives should we discuss?

Depending on your condition, alternatives may include nonsurgical care or a different operation. For selected patients, cervical disc replacement or a posterior procedure may be considered. They are not interchangeable with ACDF or suitable for everyone.

Ask: “Why does this option fit my findings and goals better than the alternatives?”

What will recovery involve?

Recovery depends on your condition, the number of levels treated, your health, and your surgeon’s plan. Discharge timing, collar use, activity limits, and follow-up are individualized.

Ask when you can drive, return to work, lift, and resume exercise, and what help you should arrange at home. Use the instructions given by your own care team rather than a general online timeline.

What should I bring to the conversation?
  • Your questions and the activities you want to get back to.
  • Information about treatments you have tried and how they helped.
  • Available imaging and a current medication list.

Ask what the procedure is expected to improve, what it may not change, and what happens if you choose to wait.
